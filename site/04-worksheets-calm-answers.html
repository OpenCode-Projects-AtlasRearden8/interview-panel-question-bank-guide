Chapter 4 — Calm-Answer Worksheets

Print this chapter. Yes, on paper if you have a printer; a text file works too. Fill-in lines are meant to be filled in by hand, in your own words, on your own machine. Nothing here gets uploaded anywhere.

The calm shape

A calm answer is:

  1. One true sentence setting the scene.
  2. One specific detail — a number, a name, a time, an object.
  3. One action you took (not "we," not "the team" — you).
  4. One ending — how it turned out, even if the ending is "not great, but

I learned X."

  1. Stop talking. Let the interviewer pick the next probe.

If your draft has more than five sentences, cut it until the shape fits. The calm answer is short on purpose: it leaves room for follow-up, which is where you actually get to be impressive.

Worksheet A — Calm Answer (candidate side)

Use one copy of this worksheet per drafted question. Three questions is plenty; Chapter 1–3 banks tell you which three to pick first.

  • The question I'm preparing for (copy it from the bank): ________
  • Scene (one sentence): ________
  • Detail (a number or name): ________
  • My action (I did…): ________
  • Ending (what happened): ________
  • Calm-close line (one sentence, say it and stop): ________
  • Energy check: On a 1–5 scale, how charged is this story for me? ________ (If it's a 4 or 5 — still angry, still raw — soften the wording or pick a different story. A calm answer cannot carry charged feelings without leaking them.)

Worksheet B — Adapt-a-Question (both sides)

When a bank question doesn't quite fit your opening, adapt it — but adapt one part at a time, so the question stays job-relevant and fair.

  • Bank question I'm adapting (copy it): ________
  • My opening's version of Scene is: ________
  • The renamed detail (tools, systems, customers we actually have): ________
  • Does the change keep the question job-relevant? (Y/N): ________
  • Does the change still work for a candidate with no experience here? (Y/N): ________
  • Did the rewrite erase the listen-for? (Y/N): ________ (The listen-for is the skill you're testing. Keep that, even when the words change.)

Worksheet C — Panel Pick (hiring side)

Pick your five questions before the interviews start — Chapter 5 explains why every candidate must get the same list.

  • Opening (Stage 1): Q________
  • Reliability or trust (Stage 2): Q________
  • Conflict or feedback (Stage 2 or 3): Q________
  • Realism or close (Stage 3 or 4): Q________
  • Wildcard if time allows: Q________
  • My scoring anchors (write one concrete line for a 2 and a 4 — see Chapter 7's score sheet): ________ / ________
  • The two listen-fors I refuse to skip, even on a bad afternoon: ________

Worksheet D — Red-Flag Softener (candidate side)

For the question you truly dread.

  • The question: ________
  • Why it stings: ________ (be honest; know your own nerve)
  • My calm-close line if it comes up anyway: "________."
  • If it's a legally risky question instead — Chapter 5 lists those — my calm redirect: "________" (e.g., "I keep my personal life and work schedule in good order, but I'm happy to talk about the availability I can commit to.")

Fairness pre-check for adaptors

Before you finalize an adapted question, check the three:

  • Is this question about the job, not the person's memory or background?
  • Would a well-rested stranger with three different skin tones, names, and family pictures get different treatment from this wording? (If yes, rewrite it until the obvious answer is no.)
  • Can every candidate in the pool answer it with the same information — or is it a question only someone who already worked here can ace?

If all three pass, you're done. Chapter 5 covers the "why" behind every rule in this list, and what to do when a question needs to change after the first candidate already heard it.
