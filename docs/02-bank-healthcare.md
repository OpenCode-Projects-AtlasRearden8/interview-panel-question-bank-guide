# Chapter 2 — Healthcare Question Bank

For hiring (or being) a **CNA, Medical Assistant, LPN/RN, Front-Desk, Tech,
Pharmacy Assistant, or Unit Clerk** — a real facility floor, front line, or
back office. Author's bias: Dylan spent years as a floor nurse before he ever
wrote a book, and this chapter is the one he wishes existed when he was
training his first orientee.

> **Not medical or legal advice.** This workbook is about *interviewing* —
> how to pick fair questions and how to answer them calmly. It does not
> teach patient care, and it does not replace your facility's HR guidelines,
> scope-of-practice rules, or state licensure requirements. When your
> organization's policy and this book disagree, policy wins.

The **Questions** entries under each heading are plain, calm versions of
classic questions — intentionally free of "What would you do if you were me"
verbal gymnastics. "When to draw the line" guidance appears inline in
**Listen for** notes, because some very common interview questions are
unfair or legally risky in healthcare hiring; the unfair patterns are
flagged there rather than in a separate section.

## Hiring side — pick your stage and open

### Stage 1 — Open and connect

- **Q: Q1. Walk me through your last shift. What did the day actually look like?**
  - **Why it's included:** You learn their real scope (vitals? med passes?
    admission paperwork?) without asking anything diagnostic or invasive.
  - **Listen for:** Rhythm and realistic pacing — "4 a.m. vitals, then
    breakfast trays, one fall in there somewhere" — rather than a recitation
    of the job description.
- **Q: Q2. What patient population have you worked with, and what was
  honestly hard about it?**
  - **Why it's included:** Population fit matters (peds vs. geriatric vs.
    psych). The *honestly hard* clause invites real talk instead of
    interview-speak.
  - **Listen for:** Named populations and a specific hard part ("dementia
    sundowning on 12-hour shifts"). Red flag: "I love all patients equally" —
    empathy with no texture.

### Stage 2 — Safety, scope, and speaking up

- **Q: Q3. Tell me about a time you caught a mistake — yours or someone
  else's. What happened next?**
  - **Why it's included:** The single most predictive safety question for
    floor staff. Behavior under an error beat attitude statements.
  - **Listen for:** They *reported* it. Strong answer includes who they told
    and what changed. Red flag: fixing it silently with no report, or blaming.
- **Q: Q4. How do you handle an order or task you think might be outside
  your scope?**
  - **Why it's included:** Scope violations hurt patients and licenses. You
    want people who check *before* doing, not after.
  - **Listen for:** A specific escalation path ("I'd ask the charge nurse
    before touching it"), not a vague "I follow my gut."
- **Q: Q5. Tell me about a time you had to speak up to someone senior.**
  - **Why it's included:** Healthcare teamwork fails quietly when junior
    staff won't push back on senior staff. You need this in the file.
  - **Listen for:** The pushback happened, respectfully, and something
    changed — or the person is able to say "I didn't, and I regret it and here
    is what I do now." That honesty is hireable. Silence is not.

### Stage 3 — Reliability, documentation, and resilience

- **Q: Q6. How do you keep from dropping small tasks during a busy shift?**
  - **Why it's included:** Healthcare floor work is a hundred tiny obligations
    (call lights, labs, family questions). This is a systems question.
  - **Listen for:** Any real system — paper brain sheet, chunked med passes,
    double-checked task list at shift change. Adapting is fine; "I just
    remember everything" is not.
- **Q: Q7. What does good documentation mean to you?**
  - **Why it's included:** Documentation is both patient-safety and
    legal-safety. Sloppy charters create liability regardless of role.
  - **Listen for:** "Time, factual, no shortcuts" plus a habit ("I chart the
    fall first, then the vitals that follow"). Red flag: "I'll back-chart it
    after rounds" as a lifestyle.
- **Q: Q8. What do you do when a shift goes sideways and it's clearly a bad
  day?**
  - **Why it's included:** Resilience question — but not the "self-care
    habits" lecture. It's about *pacing*: what they do mid-shift to keep
    functioning.
  - **Listen for:** Concrete mid-shift moves (pause, breathe, ask for
    help, debrief after) rather than platitudes ("I drink water").

### Stage 4 — Close and wrap

- **Q: Q9. What would make you quit a first month in?**
  - **Why it's included:** Same as Chapter 1's, and it matters more in
    healthcare because the job is physically and emotionally close to people.
  - **Listen for:** Safe-line answers (unsafe ratios with no report, asked to
    chart something that didn't happen, asked to perform past license).
  **Not** a red flag: "if the schedule kills me I'll ask for a transfer first."

## Candidate side — the five calm answers

### Candidate entry A — Real talk, opening

- **Q: Q10. Walk me through your last shift.**
  - **Calm-answer scaffold:**

      - A typical shift: ________ to ________.
      - The three things I did most: ________, ________, ________.
      - One thing that made the day harder than the checklist said:
        ________.

### Candidate entry B — Safety and trust

- **Q: Q11. Tell me about a mistake you caught or made.**
  - **Calm-answer scaffold:**

      - The mistake: ________ (one sentence, no drama).
      - What I noticed: ________.
      - What I did next: ________ (name the person you told, and the fix).
      - What I changed so it doesn't repeat: ________.
      - Admission line if it's a mistake-free record: "Nothing I can name, so
        here's what I *watch for* on a shift: ________."

### Candidate entry C — Scope and boundaries

- **Q: Q12. How do you handle a task that might be outside your scope?**
  - **Calm-answer scaffold:**

      - My line: tasks I've been trained and signed off on: ________.
      - When I'm unsure: I stop, and I ask ________ (charge nurse /
        supervisor / colleague) before I do it.
      - One time I actually did this: ________.

### Candidate entry D — Resilience and pacing

- **Q: Q13. What do you do when a shift goes sideways?**
  - **Calm-answer scaffold:**

      - My mid-shift reset: ________ (30 seconds in the med room / one long
        breath once I've called for help).
      - The thing I ask for when I truly need it: ________.
      - What I do *after* a rough shift: ________ (debrief, sleep, note it
        in my brain book — not doomscrolling).

### Candidate entry E — Close

- **Q: Q14. What would make you quit in the first month?**
  - **Calm-answer scaffold:**

      - Hard line: ________ (patient safety, falsified charting, unsafe task
        outside my license).
      - First thing I'd try before quitting: ________ (raise it, ask for
        preceptor change, request the staffing adjustment).

> When in doubt in a healthcare interview, pick the calm answer that names
> **one safe person you'd escalate to on day one.** Panels don't remember
> perfect paragraphs; they remember who said "I would call the charge nurse."
