# Chapter 3 — Small-Business Question Bank

For hiring (or being) the **first employee, the fifth employee, or the
whole weekend crew** at a small shop, café, clinic-back-office, barbershop,
or storefront — anywhere the job post says "wear many hats" and means it.

Small-business interviewer reality check: you probably have 20 minutes, not
50. Pick **five** questions max — two from Stage 1, two from Stage 2, one
from Stage 3 — and reuse the same five every time so you can actually
compare people to each other. Fairness note: hiring *on feel* with a
different question set per candidate is how good shops accidentally build a
team of people who all remind the owner of their nephew. Chapter 5 explains
why structure beats feel.

## Hiring side — pick your stage and open

### Stage 1 — Service and sense

- **Q: Q1. Tell me about a customer who was plainly wrong — and what you did.**
  - **Why it's included:** Small-business front lines are argued with daily.
    You're not hiring someone who never argued; you're hiring someone who
    argues *calmly and lands somewhere*.
  - **Listen for:** De-escalation plus a repair step ("I told them the policy,
    then gave them the one exception I'm allowed"). Red flag: relish. If
    they're smiling too hard while telling the story, they've told it before
    and they enjoyed it.
- **Q: Q2. What's the difference between "I don't know" and "I'll find out"
  to a customer? How do you actually handle each?**
  - **Why it's included:** Small businesses live or die on the second one.
    Some staff freeze on either; some bluff.
  - **Listen for:** "I don't know — let me find out, I'll be right back" said
    out loud with a follow-through habit. A candidate can even say the literal
    words; that's calm confidence, not weakness.

### Stage 2 — Hats, hours, money, and honesty

- **Q: Q3. This job is three jobs. Which of them do you dread most: the
  register, the books, or the floor?**
  - **Why it's included:** You get to see where the fit is before you hire,
    and the candidate gets to be honest about limits in a low-stakes setting.
  - **Listen for:** An honest pick *with a reason* ("I'd rather close out
    than deliver any pitch, because counting a till calms me down"). No-dread
    at all is a mild red flag from anyone honest.
- **Q: Q4. Tell me about a time you handled money or a till. What did you
  get wrong, and how did you fix it?**
  - **Why it's included:** There are no candidates with a perfect cash record
    who've actually worked a till. Honesty here predicts longer-term honesty.
  - **Listen for:** Ownership of a variance and the system fix (double count,
    receipt logs). Red flag (real, not a joke): "Never a problem" with no
    story at all.
- **Q: Q5. Do you want this shift-clock job because you need the hours, or
  have you thought about what the job is like?**
  - **Why it's included:** Both answers are fine, and neither is a good
    interview answer on autopilot. This one fact can also be asked directly
    — "What do you actually need this job to be?" — more honestly.
  - **Listen for:** A direct answer that matches expectations. A softener
    ("I just love working with people") is fine *in addition to*, not instead
    of, the real answer.

### Stage 3 — Close and wrap

- **Q: Q6. What would you do on Day 1 without anyone labeling the shelves
  first?**
  - **Why it's included:** Small businesses have no onboarding week, and
    nobody knows the color of the register paper. The candidate who says
    "I'd ask what the routine is and shadow for one run through it" understands
    that. The candidate who describes their own personal method doesn't yet.
  - **Listen for:** Ask-then-act. Not "I know how to laminate."

## Candidate side — the four calm answers

> Small-business interviewers usually run their questions off the top of
> their head, which means the *exact* wording in the bank may not show up.
> Practice the calm shape of these answers and you'll survive the variations.

### Candidate entry A — Service

- **Q: Q7. Tell me about a customer who was wrong — and what you did.**
  - **Calm-answer scaffold:**

      - The situation, in one sentence: ________.
      - What I said first that wasn't "you're wrong": ________.
      - What I offered inside my authority: ________.
      - How it ended: ________ calmed down / didn't — and what I told the
        owner or lead afterward: ________.

### Candidate entry B — Honesty and money

- **Q: Q8. Tell me about handling money. What did you get wrong?**
  - **Calm-answer scaffold:**

      - The honest mistake: ________ (one sentence).
      - How it got caught: ________.
      - What I did: ________ (reported, recounted, told the owner).
      - The tiny system I built so it doesn't repeat: ________.

### Candidate entry C — Hours and fit

- **Q: Q9. What do you actually need this job to be?**
  - **Calm-answer scaffold:**

      - The plain truth: I need ________ hours, and I can flex on ________.
      - What I'm good at that shows up in the first week: ________.
      - What I can't do, said calmly: ________ (not school-hiding, not
        story-spinning — just the honest line).

### Candidate entry D — Close

- **Q: Q10. What would you do on Day 1 here?**
  - **Calm-answer scaffold:**

      - I would ask one question first: ________.
      - Then shadow the routine for ________.
      - Then I do the boring thing that makes the next hour go smoother:
        ________.

> The calm answer routine for a small-business interview:
> **one scene, one action, one ending.** If your answer mentions two
> customers, a system upgrade, and a person's whole character arc, you've
> written an essay. Stop early and let them ask the follow-up.
