# Chapter 5 — Fairness Notes

This chapter is reason enough to own the workbook. Read it *before* you
finalize a question list, not after a complaint. Short version, in one
line: **same questions, same order, same score sheet, for every candidate.**
Everything else is detail.

## The core rules, in plain English

These rules trace back to U.S. guidance (EEOC) and basic study design, but
you do not need to be a lawyer to follow them. Check your own state and
local rules; Indiana readers, the LLC's home state, mostly follow the
federal lines — but never trust a book (including this one) over your own
jurisdiction.

- One **job-relevant** question set. Questions should be about the *work* —
  tasks, judgment, reliability — not about the person's life history.
- Same set for everyone in the pool. If you tweak a question for Candidate 3,
  you either tweak it **before** any interviews start, or you keep the old
  version and note why. Ad-hoc edits mid-pool are how you end up comparing
  apples to improvised oranges.
- **Structured scoring.** A one-page score sheet (Chapter 7) with anchored
  numbers, filled out *before* the next candidate walks in, beats a "gut
  feel" debrief every time it's tried.
- Write it down while it's fresh. Memory after four interviews is mush;
  a score sheet filled in the ten minutes after each interview is not.
- **No free-form probing into family, health, or beliefs.** If a candidate
  volunteers personal information, note that you didn't ask, and steer on.

## The bottom line on fairness structure

Same questions, same order, same anchors, same sheet. If an interview is
fair enough that a neutral stranger could read the sheets and reconstruct
*why* the hire happened, it's fair enough to defend. If it's only fair
because you're a nice person having a nice day — it isn't.

## Unfair questions, and what to ask instead

Questions in the left column can run afoul of discrimination rules and —
just as bad — measure nothing relevant to the job. Use the right column
instead, which gets better information anyway.

| Commonly asked | What's wrong with it | Ask instead |
|---|---|---|
| "Do you have kids? Who watches them?" | Family status is protected; the answers don't predict attendance. | "This shift starts at 7. What does your weekday morning look like?" |
| "How old are you?" / "When did you graduate?" | Age is protected; grad year is just age with extra steps. | "Walk me through your most recent role or class." |
| "Where are you from... originally?" | National origin; small talk sneaks into the file. | "What languages do you use comfortably at work, if any?" |
| "Is that a service dog? What's your condition?" | Disability probing; you don't need the diagnosis. | "Are you able to perform the essential duties of this role, with or without reasonable accommodation?" |
| "Do you believe in..." (any faith) | Religious belief is protected. Drop it entirely; it's never job-relevant. | Nothing. Not even politely. |
| "Have you ever been arrested?" | Arrest ≠ conviction; in many places it's off-limits. | Follow your jurisdiction's conviction-question rules exactly, or drop it. |
| "What did you make at your last job?" | In a growing list of places, salary history is banned. | "This range is $X–$Y. Where do you land, and what would make that work?" |
| "Would you tell me about a time you were drunk at work?" | Presumes misconduct; measures nothing. | "Tell me about a time you made a judgment call at work you'd want the owner to know about — any outcome." |

If a candidate volunteers protected information ("I'm pregnant, so..."),
the calm hire-side move is a smooth redirect back to the job: "Understood.
And the Tuesday-to-Saturday flow — would that work for you?" Don't note
the pregnancy. Note the schedule answer you'd write down for anyone.

## Fair scoring in three moves

1. **Anchor your scale before you meet anyone.** For a 1–5 scale, write one
   sentence for what a 2 looks like and one for what a 4 looks like, per
   question. Otherwise every score sheet becomes a mood ring with a grid on it.
2. **Score independently, before anyone talks.** On a two-person panel, each
   interviewer fills their own sheet in silence, then compares. Talking first
   averages the panel to the loudest voice.
3. **Keep the notes about behavior, not people.** "Couldn't name a single
   repair step in the conflict story" is fair. "Reminded me of my cousin's
   ex" is not, and it is also useless to the next panel.
